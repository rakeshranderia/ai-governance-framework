# AI Governance Framework

AI is moving too quickly for organisations to manage it effectively by simply maintaining a list of approved and prohibited tools.

This repository explores a practical approach to adopting AI while managing security, privacy, data, regulatory and operational risk.

My starting position is that AI governance should help an organisation reach an informed **"yes" or "no"** rather than making "no" the default answer.

---

## My Approach

I tend to look at AI adoption through four things:

**Use Case + Data + Risk + Platform**

### Use Case

What is someone actually trying to achieve with AI?

The risk associated with summarising public information is very different from using AI to analyse confidential organisational data or make a significant business decision.

### Data

What information will be provided to or processed by the AI system?

This needs to consider:

- Data classification
- Personal information
- Client information
- Commercially sensitive information
- Intellectual property
- Regulatory requirements
- Data loss

### Risk

What could happen if the data was exposed, the model behaved unexpectedly or the output was wrong?

The level of governance should reflect the potential consequence.

### Platform

What do we know about the AI service being proposed?

This may include:

- Security controls
- Privacy arrangements
- Data handling
- Enterprise protections
- Access management
- Logging and monitoring
- Model or service architecture

Looking at these four areas together provides a more useful assessment than simply asking:

**"Is this AI product approved?"**

---

## Governance Model

A practical governance lifecycle is:

**Identify → Assess → Govern → Implement → Monitor → Improve**

The important part is proportionality.

A low-risk productivity use case should not necessarily require the same governance as an AI system processing sensitive information or influencing significant decisions.

Governance needs to reflect both the **use case and its potential impact**.

## Managing Shadow AI

One of the practical challenges organisations face is that employees can access AI services faster than traditional technology governance processes can assess them.

Simply blocking every new service is unlikely to be a sustainable strategy.

Equally, allowing unrestricted use can create risks around:

- Sensitive information
- Privacy
- Intellectual property
- Data loss
- Quality
- Security
- Regulatory obligations

A more sustainable approach is to give employees a clear pathway for proposing AI use cases and give the organisation a consistent way of assessing them.

The objective is to move from:

**Unmanaged AI Adoption**

to:

**Visible → Assessed → Controlled → Measured AI Adoption**

---

## Acceptable Use

An AI acceptable-use approach should help employees understand both what they can do and where additional review is required.

This can include guidance around:

- Approved AI services
- Permitted information
- Restricted information
- Data classification
- Human review of AI output
- Privacy
- Security
- Intellectual property
- High-risk use cases
- Escalation and approval

Good acceptable-use guidance should make responsible adoption easier, not simply create another policy employees need to work around.

## Measuring AI

AI governance should not only measure risk.

Organisations also need to understand whether AI is creating value.

Useful areas to consider include:

**Adoption**

Are employees actually using the capability?

**Productivity**

Is it reducing effort or improving how work is performed?

**Efficiency**

Are there repeatable activities where AI improves turnaround or capacity?

**Risk**

Are AI use cases operating within agreed controls?

**Data**

Is organisational information being handled appropriately?

**Quality**

Are outputs sufficiently reliable for the intended use?

The purpose of measurement is not simply to prove that AI is being used.

It is to understand whether it is being used **safely and usefully**.

## From Framework to Practice

I have also documented a practical example of applying these principles within an organisation of approximately 120 people.

The initiative involved an AI pilot of around 40 staff exploring real business use cases including:

- Tender responses
- Data analysis and statistical modelling
- Coding assistance and QA

The broader objective was to understand how AI could be enabled without allowing it to develop into uncontrolled Shadow IT.

This included developing approaches around use-case risk, data classification, privacy, data loss and consistent assessment across different AI platforms.

## Perspective

I don't believe successful AI governance is primarily about controlling a particular product.

The products will change.

The models will change.

The use cases will change.

The more sustainable capability is having a consistent way to ask:

**What are we trying to do?**

**What data is involved?**

**What could go wrong?**

**What controls are available?**

**And is the remaining risk acceptable?**

That allows governance to evolve with the technology rather than constantly trying to catch up with it.

The goal is not unrestricted AI adoption.

Nor is it preventing AI adoption.

It is creating enough structure for the organisation to **experiment, learn and adopt AI responsibly.**
